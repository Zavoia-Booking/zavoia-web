# Classic SEO Rules Now Gate AI Answers

As of 12 November 2026, every primary source in scope judges a server-rendered English–Romanian marketplace on the same evidence: the raw HTML each URL returns, meaning its status code, the signals in a valid `<head>`, and the visible text in its first bytes. Google states that there are **"no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode"** beyond being indexed and eligible for a snippet, calls optimizing for generative AI "still SEO", ignores llms.txt and asks for no special schema ([Google, AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features); [Google, AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)), while Microsoft says Bing, Copilot and its grounding API share "the same core crawling, indexing, and ranking foundation" ([Bing Webmaster Guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a)). Beyond Google's renderer, the fetchers behind ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and Copilot were measured reading raw HTML without executing JavaScript, so the server response, in document order, is what they can quote ([Vercel/MERJ](https://vercel.com/blog/the-rise-of-the-ai-crawler); [Advanced Web Ranking](https://www.advancedwebranking.com/blog/what-ai-search-crawlers-read)). The core rulebook (a 200 status, one absolute self-referencing canonical, reciprocal `en`/`ro` hreflang with an `x-default` fallback, crawlable `<a href>` links, canonical-only sitemaps, markup that matches visible text) did not change in 2025–2026, but its details did: Googlebot now reads only the **first 2 MB of uncompressed HTML** instead of 15 MB, JavaScript must not alter a canonical or a `noindex`, non-200 pages may never be rendered, **FAQ rich results ended on 7 May 2026**, breadcrumbs show only on desktop, hreflang codes became case-insensitive, and SVG-only favicons fall outside Google's format list. Site owners gained finer AI switches, including a Search Console "Search generative AI" control live worldwide since **31 August 2026** and separate search, training and user-fetch tokens at OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity and Apple, while one edge change raised the stakes: since **15 September 2026** a Cloudflare "Block" on AI training also blocks Googlebot, Bingbot and Applebot ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/accountable-mixed-use-ai-crawlers/)). The largest open question for a site that streams with React Suspense is how HTML-only AI fetchers treat text that arrives late inside `<div hidden>`: no vendor documents it and no public test covers it.

## A 200 response and one consistent canonical decide what gets indexed

Google's indexing gate is blunt: **"Google only indexes pages that are served with an HTTP `200 (success)` status code"** ([Google, Technical requirements](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/technical)), and even a 200 "doesn't guarantee indexing" ([Google, HTTP status codes](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/troubleshooting/http-status-codes)). Everything else in this layer exists to make sure that, for each piece of content, the one 200 URL Google keeps is the one the site intends, and that every other address either redirects to it, points at it, or returns an honest error.

### Canonicals are stacked hints that JavaScript may no longer rewrite

A canonical declaration is "a hint, not a rule" ([Google, What is canonicalization](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/canonicalization)). Google ranks the signals explicitly: **redirects and `rel="canonical"` are "strong", sitemap inclusion is "weak"**, and the methods "stack", so combining them raises the odds that the preferred URL wins ([Google, Consolidate duplicate URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls)). The canonical is crawled most often, duplicates less, and Google evaluates quality on the canonical ([Google, What is canonicalization](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/canonicalization)). The `link` element counts only inside a valid `<head>`, and validity is fragile: **the first invalid element, such as an `img` or `iframe`, makes Google assume the head has ended and ignore every `meta` and `link` after it**, canonical and hreflang included ([Google, Valid page metadata](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/valid-page-metadata)). Robots meta tags are the exception, because since a documentation update of 24 March 2026 Google says it "will respect robots meta tags in the body section of an HTML document as well" ([Google, Robots meta tag spec](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag)).

The mechanics are prescriptive. Use absolute URLs (Google's own "Bad example" is a root-relative path), choose one method rather than both a `Link` header and a `link` element, keep a self-referencing canonical on the canonical page, link internally to the canonical, and never name one URL in the sitemap and another in `rel="canonical"`; Google also rules out `noindex`, robots.txt, the URL removal tool and URL fragments as canonicalization tools ([Google, Consolidate duplicate URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls)). The 2025 change concerns JavaScript. On **17 December 2025** Google documented that "Canonicalization happens before and after rendering", so the canonical in the server HTML must equal any value JavaScript sets, or be left out of the server HTML entirely ([Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates); [Google, JavaScript SEO basics](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/javascript-seo-basics)). Multiple or conflicting canonical tags "may lead to unexpected results", and a declared canonical that is not similar to the page will never be chosen: "A duplicate page must be similar to the canonical. (That's what duplicate means.)" ([Google, Page indexing report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7440203)). After a fix, Google "might hold pages in a duplicate cluster for up to two weeks", a sentence added on 10 July 2026 ([Google, Fix canonicalization issues](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/canonicalization-troubleshooting)).

For a marketplace with slugs, IDs, filters and campaign links, three URL-hygiene rules carry most of the weight. Google's URL handling **"is case sensitive"**, so `/APPLE` and `/apple` are distinct URLs, and a server that treats them alike should "convert all text to the same case" ([Google, URL structure](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/url-structure)). Parameters that do not change content (referral tags, sort orders, session IDs) should be trimmed, and signals pointing at a `?gclid=` variant consolidate to the canonical once Google picks it ([Google, URL structure](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/url-structure); [Google, Consolidate duplicate URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls)). Duplicates that should disappear, such as numeric-ID aliases of slug URLs, case variants or a non-preferred host, call for a permanent redirect, which Google reserves for "deprecating a duplicate page" ([Google, Consolidate duplicate URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls)). One gap deserves an audit flag: **no Google page says what happens when `rel="canonical"` points at a 404, a redirect or a `noindex` URL**. Because Google indexes only 200 pages and requires a similar target, such a declaration cannot win and Google falls back to its own choice. That is an inference from the documentation, so the audit standard is to treat any non-200 canonical target as a defect, not as a tolerable hint.

### Only permanent server-side redirects consolidate cleanly

Google treats **301 and 308 as strong signals and 302, 303 and 307 as weak ones**, follows up to 10 hops, and ignores any content served by the redirecting URL ([Google, HTTP status codes](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/troubleshooting/http-status-codes)). Permanent redirects put the target in search results, temporary ones keep the source ([Google, Redirects](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/301-redirects)). A zero-second meta refresh and a JavaScript `location` change also count as permanent, but Google says to use JavaScript redirects only when server-side or meta-refresh redirects are impossible, because "rendering may fail" ([Google, Redirects](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/301-redirects)). One wording change retires an old checklist assumption: the consolidation page once said that "All redirection methods —`301` and `302` redirects, `meta-refresh`, JavaScript redirects—have the same effect", and it now says only "All permanent redirection methods" do ([Google, Consolidate duplicate URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls)). Sitemaps should list final targets, because Search Console's sitemap report recommends replacing redirecting URLs "with the URLs that should actually be crawled" ([Google, Sitemaps report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7451001)).

### Error pages need real status codes because Google may never run their scripts

A **soft 404** is a 200 response that tells users the page does not exist, or a page with "no main content"; Google's examples include "An empty internal search result page" and "An unloaded or otherwise missing JavaScript file", detection uses the rendered result, and such pages "are excluded from Search" ([Google, Troubleshoot crawling errors](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/troubleshoot-crawling-errors)). Since **18 December 2025** Google also says that for non-200 responses "rendering might be skipped" ([Google, JavaScript SEO basics](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/javascript-seo-basics)). That cuts both ways: a real 404 page's client-side code may never run, and a page that relies on injecting `noindex` with JavaScript starts life as an indexable 200 whose fate depends on rendering succeeding. Google offers the JavaScript redirect to a 404 URL and the injected `noindex` only for client-side routing, where status codes are "impossible or impractical" ([Google, JavaScript SEO basics](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/javascript-seo-basics)); a server-rendered app should return the code itself, since "Custom 404 pages are created solely for users" ([Google, Troubleshoot crawling errors](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/troubleshoot-crawling-errors)). A `prerender-status-code` meta tag does nothing ("Googlebot currently ignores that status code", per Martin Splitt in office hours, which are not documentation) ([Google office hours, December 2023](https://developers.google.com/search/help/office-hours/2023/december)). Listing pages carry a specific rule: **return 404 when a filter combination has no results**, contains duplicate or nonsensical filters, or asks for a nonexistent page number, and serve it at the requested URL rather than redirecting to a shared error page ([Google, Faceted navigation](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/faceted-navigation)). On a local-services marketplace, a city-and-service page with zero providers is exactly that case.

The remaining codes behave as Google's crawling documentation describes. All 4xx codes except 429 keep new URLs out of the index and remove indexed ones, "Newly encountered `404` pages aren't processed", and 401 or 403 must not be used to slow crawling ([Google, HTTP status codes](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/troubleshooting/http-status-codes)). Removed pages should return 404 or 410 and moved pages a 301 ([Google, Troubleshoot crawling errors](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/troubleshoot-crawling-errors)), and soft 404s "will continue to be crawled, and waste your budget" ([Google, Crawl budget](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawl-budget)). **5xx and 429 responses slow crawling; already indexed URLs survive for a while "but eventually" drop**, and URLs that persistently return server errors are removed ([Google, HTTP status codes](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/troubleshooting/http-status-codes)). Returning 500, 503 or 429 is an accepted emergency brake for "a couple of hours, or 1-2 days", optionally with a `Retry-After` header (details added 6 October 2026), but held for longer the URLs "may be dropped from Google's index" ([Google, Reduce crawl rate](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/reduce-crawl-rate)).

### Robots.txt controls crawling while robots meta controls indexing and snippets

Google is explicit that robots.txt "is not a mechanism for keeping a web page out of Google" ([Google, Introduction to robots.txt](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots/intro)). A Disallow hides any on-page `noindex`, so a blocked URL can still be indexed from links without its content; Search Console's fix for "Indexed, though blocked by robots.txt" is to "remove the robots.txt block and use 'noindex'", and a `noindex` line inside robots.txt is unsupported ([Google, Page indexing report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7440203); [Google, Block indexing](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/block-indexing)). The file must sit at the root of each host, protocol and port, be UTF-8, and stay under **500 KiB**; Google caches it for up to 24 hours, reads a 4xx other than 429 as "no crawl restrictions", and on a 5xx stops crawling for 12 hours, then uses the last good copy for up to 30 days ([Google, robots.txt spec](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/robots-txt/robots-txt-spec)). **Only `user-agent`, `allow`, `disallow` and `sitemap` are supported**; `crawl-delay`, `host` and every other unlisted field are ignored, a point Google made explicit in October 2024 ([Google, robots.txt spec](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/robots-txt/robots-txt-spec); [Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)).

Group selection is where AI-crawler rules usually go wrong. A crawler obeys only the single most specific group that matches it, and "User agent specific groups and global groups (`*`) are not combined", so a `User-agent: Googlebot` group replaces rather than extends the `*` rules for Google; the spec calls group order "irrelevant", contradicting the "top to bottom" wording on Google's how-to page, and the spec is the normative text ([Google, robots.txt spec](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/robots-txt/robots-txt-spec); [Google, Create a robots.txt file](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/robots-txt/create-robots-txt)). The longest matching path wins, ties go to the least restrictive rule, paths are case-sensitive, `*` and `$` are the only wildcards, and `*` does not cover Google's AdsBot crawlers ([Google, robots.txt spec](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/robots-txt/robots-txt-spec); [Google, Create a robots.txt file](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/robots-txt/create-robots-txt)). Since 23 October 2026 the spec also states that rule paths are compared in percent-encoded form and recommends percent-encoding them, which matters for any path containing Romanian diacritics ([Google, robots.txt spec](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/robots-txt/robots-txt-spec)).

On the page, Google honours `all`, `noindex`, `nofollow`, `none`, `nosnippet`, `indexifembedded`, `max-snippet`, `max-image-preview`, `max-video-preview`, `notranslate`, `noimageindex` and `unavailable_after`; rules are case-insensitive, conflicting rules resolve to the most restrictive, and rules addressed to different crawlers add up ([Google, Robots meta tag spec](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag)). **`noarchive`, `nocache` and `nositelinkssearchbox` are dead for Google** but not for Bing, which uses the first two to govern Copilot. `data-nosnippet` works only on `span`, `div` and `section`, and Google extracts it "both before and after rendering", so it belongs in the server HTML rather than being toggled by script ([Google, Robots meta tag spec](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag)). One behaviour is formally undefined: John Mueller said in 2024 office hours that the long-term outcome of `noindex, follow` "is not defined, and will change over the site's lifetime", so it is not a dependable way to pass discovery through thin listing pages ([Google office hours, April 2024](https://developers.google.com/search/help/office-hours/2024/april)).

The same directive means different things to each engine, which is why an audit should read page-level controls per vendor:

| Directive | Google Search, incl. AI Overviews and AI Mode | Bing, Copilot and grounding API | Apple (Spotlight, Siri, Safari) |
|---|---|---|---|
| `noindex` | Not shown in results | Removed from Bing, Copilot and grounding results | Supported |
| `nosnippet` | No snippet, and content not used as "direct input" for AI Overviews and AI Mode | No captions; "may limit Copilot citation quality" | No description or web answer; not used as AI answer context |
| `max-snippet:[n]` | Caps snippet length and AI input; structured data exempt except creative-work `description` values | Not documented | Not documented |
| `data-nosnippet` | Excluded from snippets; valid on `span`, `div`, `section` only | Supported since 15 Oct 2025; excluded from snippets and AI summaries but still ranks | Not documented |
| `noarchive` | Ignored | Keeps content out of Copilot answers and grounding results | Not documented |
| `nocache` | Ignored | Copilot limited to URL, title and snippet | Not documented |

The rows come from Google's robots meta specification ([Google](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag)), the Bing Webmaster Guidelines and Bing's `data-nosnippet` announcement ([Bing guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a); [Bing, data-nosnippet](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2025/10/Bing-Introduces-Support-for-the-data-nosnippet-HTML-Attribute/)), and Apple's Applebot page ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/119829)).

### Sitemaps list canonical URLs with honest dates and nothing else

A sitemap holds at most **50 MB uncompressed or 50,000 URLs**, must be UTF-8 with fully qualified URLs, and covers only descendants of its directory unless submitted in Search Console; an index file holds up to 50,000 `loc` entries and a site may submit up to 500 index files ([Google, Build a sitemap](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/build-sitemap); [Google, Sitemap index files](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/large-sitemaps)). **Google ignores `<priority>` and `<changefreq>`**, and so does Bing ([Bing, Sitemaps in AI-powered search](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2025/7/Keeping-Content-Discoverable-with-Sitemaps-in-AI-Powered-Search/)). Google uses `<lastmod>` only when it is "consistently and verifiably" accurate and reflects a "significant" change to main content, structured data or links, not a copyright year ([Google, Build a sitemap](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/build-sitemap)), so a build timestamp stamped on every URL teaches Google to distrust the field. The sitemap should contain the URLs the site wants "to see in Google's search results", which are its canonicals, because "All pages listed in a sitemap are suggested as canonicals" ([Google, Build a sitemap](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/build-sitemap); [Google, Consolidate duplicate URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls)), and submitting URLs that are blocked, noindexed or behind a login is a documented cause of Page indexing error spikes ([Google, Page indexing report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7440203)). Submission "is merely a hint"; Google retries a failing sitemap "for a few days" and then stops until it is resubmitted, respects robots.txt when fetching sitemaps, and does not forget a sitemap's URLs when it is deleted from Search Console ([Google, Build a sitemap](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/build-sitemap); [Google, Sitemaps report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7451001)). Image sitemaps still work with only `<image:image>` and `<image:loc>` ([Google, Image sitemaps](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/image-sitemaps)).

Bing adds freshness levers Google does not use: accurate `lastmod`, ETags and **IndexNow** notifications, which Microsoft ties directly to fewer outdated URLs in Copilot answers ([Bing guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a)). An IndexNow 200 response means only that the URL was received; a submission of up to 10,000 URLs is shared among participating engines and counts against crawl quota, and Google is not among the endpoints the protocol's FAQ lists ([IndexNow documentation](https://www.indexnow.org/documentation); [IndexNow FAQ](https://www.indexnow.org/faq)).

## English and Romanian need two crawlable, reciprocal URL sets

Google's multilingual guidance starts from how Googlebot crawls. It "usually originates from the USA", sends **no `Accept-Language` header**, and does not vary its location to discover variants, so content swapped on one URL by cookie, browser language or IP address can go unseen ([Google, Multi-regional and multilingual sites](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites); [Google, Locale-adaptive pages](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/locale-adaptive-pages)). Google therefore recommends a separate URL per language, tells sites to "Avoid automatically redirecting users from one language version of a site to a different language version", says "Don't use IP analysis to adapt your content", rates locale parameters such as `?loc=de` "Not recommended", ignores geotargeting meta tags, and suggests visible links between language versions ([Google, Multi-regional and multilingual sites](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites)); robots meta tags and robots.txt "must specify the same rules in each locale" ([Google, Locale-adaptive pages](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/locale-adaptive-pages)). For an English-default site with a `/ro` section, a root URL that redirects by `Accept-Language` would show Googlebot only the English variant; the documented pattern is a non-redirecting default page annotated as `x-default`, with each language at its own crawlable URL.

### Hreflang works only between reciprocal, indexable, self-canonical URLs

Each language version **"must list itself as well as all other language versions"** with fully qualified URLs, and "If two pages don't both point to each other, the tags will be ignored" ([Google, Localized versions](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions)); John Mueller has said Google drops "just that connection" and the cluster survives with the rest ([Google office hours, November 2022](https://developers.google.com/search/help/office-hours/2022/november)). The HTML `<link>` set inside a well-formed `<head>`, the HTTP `Link:` header and sitemap `xhtml:link` entries are "equivalent", using more than one has "no benefit in Search", and hreflang must not share a `<link>` tag with attributes such as `media` ([Google, Localized versions](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions)). The `x-default` value covers unmatched languages and "will work best" on language selectors ([Google, Localized versions](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions)). Codes are ISO 639-1 languages, so `en` and `ro`, with an optional ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 region or ISO 15924 script; codes outside those standards, such as `es-419`, are unsupported, a country code alone is invalid, reserved codes such as `UK` or `EU` are ignored, and since a 21 September 2026 edit Google states that **"The `hreflang` value is case-insensitive"** ([Google, Localized versions](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions)). Hreflang is not a language detector: Google uses algorithms on visible content and ignores `lang` attributes and the URL for that purpose ([Google, Multi-regional and multilingual sites](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites)).

The documentation is silent on broken targets, so office hours fill the gap. Gary Illyes said clusters form only from validated links and **"If one of the links is `noindex`, then that won't be eligible for getting into the cluster"** ([Google office hours, January 2023](https://developers.google.com/search/help/office-hours/2023/january)), and Google discards the content of 4xx and 5xx responses outright ([Google, HTTP status codes](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/troubleshooting/http-status-codes)). The canonical must be "in the same language" as the hreflang page, Google prefers URLs inside hreflang clusters when it picks canonicals, and a sentence added between July and August 2026 tells sites with same-language regional variants to "use both canonicalization and `hreflang`" ([Google, Consolidate duplicate URLs](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls); [Google, What is canonicalization](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/canonicalization)). The audit standard follows directly, though Google never writes it as a single rule: **every hreflang URL should be a 200, indexable, self-canonical page**, because a target that redirects, errors, carries `noindex` or canonicalizes elsewhere either drops out of the cluster or contradicts it. Search Console will not verify this, since "Alternate language pages are not detected by Search Console" in the Page indexing report ([Google, Page indexing report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7440203)).

### Template-only translation turns English pages into duplicates

The rule most likely to surprise a bilingual marketplace is Google's definition of a duplicate: **language versions "are considered duplicates only if the primary content is in the same language"**, for example when "only the header, footer, and other non-critical text is translated, but the body remains the same" ([Google, What is canonicalization](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/canonicalization)). Provider descriptions, service menus and customer reviews that exist only in Romanian sit inside an English template on the English URL, so those page pairs are duplicates by definition. Google still recommends hreflang for exactly this "translate only the template" case ([Google, Localized versions](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions)), but it will cluster each pair, pick one canonical, and show the alternate by user language, as Martin Splitt described: "other URLs can and will show up in the search results, depending on the user's language and region" ([Google office hours, July 2023](https://developers.google.com/search/help/office-hours/2023/july)). Google also warns that boilerplate-only translation "can create a bad user experience if the same content appears multiple times in search results with various boilerplate languages" ([Google, Multi-regional and multilingual sites](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites)), and its title-link documentation adds a second consequence: when a `<title>` "does not match the writing system or language of the page's primary content", Google "may choose a different text as the title link" ([Google, Title links](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link)). Google publishes no threshold for how much must be translated before pages stop being duplicates. Machine translation is no longer singled out either: on **11 June 2025** Google removed its advice to block automatically translated pages with robots.txt ([Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)), leaving the spam policy's target of pages generated through "automated transformations like synonymizing, translating, or other obfuscation techniques ... where little value is provided to users" ([Google, Spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)).

Several appearance features cannot be localized by subdirectory at all. Google supports "one site name per site", defined by domain or subdomain, and the `WebSite` markup that states the preference must sit on the root home page, because "`https://example.com/de/index.html` isn't the home page" ([Google, Site names](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/site-names)). Favicons are likewise one per hostname ([Google, Favicon](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/favicon-in-search)). Open Graph's `og:locale` takes the `language_TERRITORY` format (`ro_RO`, for instance), with `og:locale:alternate` naming the other language ([Open Graph protocol](https://ogp.me/)), but Google documents no use of either tag.

## Googlebot renders the page, but most AI fetchers read only raw bytes

### Google renders every 200 page, but only the first 2 MB and never after a click

Google crawls, queues every 200 page for rendering in an evergreen headless Chromium unless a robots meta tag or header says not to index it, and indexes the rendered HTML, while still advising that "server-side or pre-rendering is still a great idea because it makes your website faster for users and crawlers, and not all bots can run JavaScript" ([Google, JavaScript SEO basics](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/javascript-seo-basics)). Rendering "is not guaranteed" ([Google, Robots meta tag spec](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag)); the renderer keeps no cookies or storage between page loads, refuses WebSockets and permission prompts, and may ignore caching headers ([Google, Fix JavaScript problems](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/fix-search-javascript)); and dynamic rendering is a deprecated workaround ([Google, Dynamic rendering](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/dynamic-rendering)). On **15 December 2025** Google softened "skips rendering" to "may skip rendering" for pages carrying `noindex`, calling the behaviour "not well defined" and telling sites that might want a page indexed not to put `noindex` in the original page code ([Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)).

The sharpest change came on **3 February 2026**: "Googlebot crawls the first 2MB of a supported file type", the limit is "applied on the uncompressed data", and past it Googlebot "only sends the already downloaded part of the file for indexing consideration" ([Google, Googlebot](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/googlebot)). The old figure was 15 MB, which remains the default for Google's other crawlers and fetchers ([Google, Crawler overview](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/overview-google-crawlers)). On a streamed React page, the late Suspense segments and the inline component payload sit at the end of the byte stream, so they are the first content an oversized document loses, and large serialized state placed early can push the main content past the cut-off.

Google draws the hidden-content line at interaction, not at visibility. Its spam policy lists "Accordion or tabbed content that toggle between hiding and showing additional content", sliders, tooltips and screen-reader text as legitimate ([Google, Spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)), and its mobile guidance suggests moving content into accordions or tabs rather than removing it, but **"Google won't load content that requires user interactions (for example, swiping, clicking, or typing) to load"** ([Google, Mobile-first indexing](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/mobile/mobile-sites-mobile-first-indexing)). An addition on 20 April 2026 sets a higher bar for "read more" deep links in snippets: content should be "immediately visible on the page to a human (and not hidden behind an expandable section or tabbed interface, for example)", scripts should not set the scroll position on load, and History API calls must not strip the hash fragment ([Google, Snippets](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/snippet)). No Google page addresses the HTML `hidden` attribute or content moved into place by an inline script; the nearest guidance is that Google flattens and indexes what the rendered DOM contains.

### Discovery needs real anchors and stable listing URLs

**Google extracts links only from `<a>` elements with an `href`**; router attributes, `onclick` handlers and `javascript:` URLs are not crawled, while script-inserted anchors with proper markup are ([Google, Link best practices](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable)). Client-side routing must use the History API rather than fragments ([Google, JavaScript SEO basics](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/javascript-seo-basics)). Google's crawlers don't "click" buttons, so paginated listings need `<a href>` links to the next page, a unique URL per page such as `?page=n`, and a canonical on each page pointing to itself rather than to page one; Google "no longer uses" `rel="next"` and `rel="prev"`, although "other search engines" may ([Google, Pagination](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/pagination-and-incremental-page-loading)). Infinite scroll needs a persistent URL per chunk with absolute page numbers, sequential links and History API URL updates ([Google, Lazy loading](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/javascript/lazy-loading)). For faceted navigation, Google's preferred options are to disallow filter URLs in robots.txt, leaving item pages and an unfiltered listing crawlable, or to keep filters in URL fragments; `rel="canonical"` and `nofollow` are "generally less effective in the long term", and facets meant for indexing need `&` separators, a fixed filter order, no duplicate filters and 404s for empty results ([Google, Faceted navigation](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/faceted-navigation)). City, service and filter combinations are a marketplace's classic infinite URL space, so the audit question is which combinations are deliberate landing pages and whether every other one is blocked or returns 404.

### Mobile-first parity breaks where components mount only on desktop

Google indexes and ranks the version fetched by Googlebot Smartphone, which makes the majority of crawl requests, and robots.txt cannot address the smartphone and desktop crawlers separately ([Google, Googlebot](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/googlebot)). The detailed parity checklist (same primary content, headings, title and meta description, alt text, structured data and robots meta) formally covers dynamic serving and separate mobile URLs, because with responsive design "the content and the metadata are the same" ([Google, Mobile-first indexing](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/mobile/mobile-sites-mobile-first-indexing)). That assumption breaks wherever React mounts a component, a link block or a JSON-LD script only above a desktop breakpoint: **"Only the content shown on the mobile site is used for indexing"**, and even equivalent content in a different DOM "can result in Google understanding the content differently" ([Google, Mobile-first indexing](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/mobile/mobile-sites-mobile-first-indexing)).

### Outside Google, the raw HTML is the page

Only Google and Apple document rendering, and Apple's wording is that Applebot "may render the content of your website within a browser" ([Apple, About Applebot](https://support.apple.com/en-us/119829)). Microsoft lists "Hiding critical content behind client-side rendering" among practices to avoid, warning that content which cannot be reliably rendered "may not be indexed or selected for grounding results" ([Bing guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a)), and a Microsoft Advertising post by a Bing product manager adds: "Don't hide important answers in tabs or expandable menus: AI systems may not render hidden content" ([Microsoft Advertising](https://about.ads.microsoft.com/en/blog/post/october-2025/optimizing-your-content-for-inclusion-in-ai-search-answers)). Anthropic documents that its API web-fetch tool "does not support websites dynamically rendered with JavaScript" and truncates content past a token limit ([Anthropic, Web fetch tool](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/web-fetch-tool)), though that page covers the developer tool rather than the consumer `Claude-User` fetcher. OpenAI's and Perplexity's crawler pages say nothing about JavaScript at all ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots); [Perplexity](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/resources/perplexity-crawlers)).

Independent measurements fill the silence, with caveats. Vercel and MERJ's December 2024 log study found that **"none of the major AI crawlers currently render JavaScript"**, covering OpenAI's three agents, ClaudeBot and PerplexityBot; ChatGPT and Claude crawlers fetched script files without executing them, Common Crawl did not render, AppleBot and Gemini (through Googlebot's infrastructure) did, and "delayed React Server Components" present in the initial HTML "may still be indexed" ([Vercel/MERJ](https://vercel.com/blog/the-rise-of-the-ai-crawler)). A summer 2026 decoy test, known only through Advanced Web Ranking's write-up, found ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity and Copilot all reporting the raw-HTML decoy rather than the JavaScript value, with Gemini's indexed answers able to reflect Googlebot's rendering while its cold live fetch did not ([Advanced Web Ranking](https://www.advancedwebranking.com/blog/what-ai-search-crawlers-read)). A March 2026 Writesonic test found CSS-hidden text (`display:none`, `visibility:hidden`, `opacity:0`) read by five or six of six assistants, pseudo-element text read by none, and JSON-LD, meta descriptions and Open Graph tags read by none, with `<title>` the only metadata that reliably got through ([Writesonic](https://writesonic.com/blog/ai-crawler-study-what-llms-see-on-your-website)). The tests disagree on Copilot (Writesonic says it ran JavaScript after about 500 milliseconds, the decoy test says it fetched the script and never ran it), and all of them are small, so they establish direction rather than settled behaviour.

### React streaming puts late content where AI fetchers are weakest

These findings collide with how React streams. In the Next.js 16.4.1 installed in this repository, a resolved Suspense boundary arrives as a `<div hidden id="S:0">` plus a script that swaps it into place ([Next.js streaming guide, local copy](../../../node_modules/next/dist/docs/01-app/02-guides/streaming.md)). The framework keeps an "HTML-limited bots" list, which includes Bingbot, BingPreview, Applebot, Google's non-Googlebot crawlers, Chrome-Lighthouse and link-preview bots such as Twitterbot and facebookexternalhit, and **contains no token for GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, ChatGPT-User, ClaudeBot, Claude-SearchBot, Claude-User, PerplexityBot, Perplexity-User or CCBot** ([Next.js html-bots.ts](https://github.com/vercel/next.js/blob/canary/packages/next/src/shared/lib/router/utils/html-bots.ts)); a check of the installed `html-bots.js` confirms the same list. For bots on that list, metadata is rendered blocking so that it lands in `<head>` (local copy of the `generateMetadata` reference, "Streaming metadata" section).

**Correction from the audit's own captures (12 November 2026).** Being on that list does not change where the page body goes. On a location page whose first, uncached render was requested by Bingbot (`x-nextjs-cache: MISS`), the response still carried the h1, the body text and the JSON-LD inside `<div hidden id="S:0">` after the footer, with the same structure that Chrome, Googlebot and GPTBot received. The out-of-order placement comes from React's size rule (a completed boundary is written out of line once the flushed bytes plus the boundary exceed `progressiveChunkSize`, 12,800 by default), not from the user agent. Evidence and source lines are in `docs/audits/2026-11-12-seo-aeo-geo-audit.md`. Googlebot is classed as JavaScript-capable and receives the streamed response, and even for listed bots the guide notes that with Cache Components "the remaining page content can still stream" ([Next.js streaming guide, local copy](../../../node_modules/next/dist/docs/01-app/02-guides/streaming.md)).

For an HTML-only AI fetcher, streamed text is present in the bytes but sits after the footer, detached from its heading, inside an element whose `hidden` attribute Mozilla's widely reused Readability extractor treats as invisible and deletes ([Readability.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mozilla/readability/main/Readability.js)), and it is the first text lost to any byte or token budget. The same code suggests that collapsed content in a native `<details>` element, which carries no `hidden` attribute, survives such filters, an inference from the extractor's source rather than a tested AI result. No AI vendor documents its extraction pipeline, so the effect of out-of-order streaming is unknown rather than proven harmful. Adding AI user agents to the framework's `htmlLimitedBots` setting would only move metadata into `<head>`; the audit's captures show it does not change body order on these routes, so it is not a remedy for this problem. Any fix should keep bot and user HTML the same, since Bing treats materially different bot content as cloaking ([Bing guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a)). The audit test the measurements imply is concrete: fetch each key URL with each AI user agent and check whether the primary text appears in the raw response, where it sits relative to its heading, whether it is inside a `hidden` element, and how many uncompressed bytes precede it. The accompanying audit ran exactly that test against a production build of the site.

## Google writes titles, snippets and site names from signals you align

### Title links and snippets come from page content, not length formulas

Google's title links are "completely automated" and draw on nine sources: the `<title>`, the main visual title, headings such as `<h1>`, `og:title` (listed since 26 August 2024), other large text, other page text, on-page anchor text, inbound link text and `WebSite` structured data ([Google, Title links](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link); [Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)). **There is "no limit" on `<title>` length**; results truncate to device width, so 50–60-character rules are third-party heuristics ([Google, Title links](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link)). The page asks for a distinct title per page, no keyword repetition, no "boilerplate" titles that differ by a single variable, the site name once "at the beginning or end", and a clearly dominant main heading, since with several prominent headings Google "may use the first heading"; for domain-level site names Google "may omit the site name from the title link" when it repeats the name already displayed ([Google, Title links](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/title-link)). A template that appends the brand twice therefore wastes truncation space and contradicts the guidance, even though no rule names that exact pattern.

Snippets come "primarily" from page content, with the meta description used "when it describes the page better"; **meta descriptions have no length limit**, should be unique per page, and may be generated programmatically if they are "human-readable and diverse" ([Google, Snippets](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/snippet)). Google says nothing about `og:description`. Snippet eligibility now doubles as AI eligibility, because Google requires a page to be "eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet" before it can be a supporting link in AI Overviews or AI Mode ([Google, AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)); a site-wide `nosnippet` added to keep text out of AI also removes classic snippets.

### Site names and favicons live at the root, and SVG alone no longer qualifies

The site name is chosen automatically from home-page content and web references, and **`WebSite` structured data on the home page is "most important"** for stating a preference, ahead of `og:site_name`, `<title>` and headings ([Google, Site names](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/site-names)). The markup requires `name` and `url` (the canonical home page), recommends an ordered `alternateName` list, must sit on the domain or subdomain root (subdirectories are unsupported), needs a crawlable home page, and should live in a single `WebSite` node repeated on any duplicate home pages; the Rich Results Test does not support site names, so validation runs through a schema validator ([Google, Site names](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/site-names)). `Organization` markup should use the same `name` and `alternateName` ([Google, Organization](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/organization)). The sitelinks search box that once justified a `SearchAction` inside `WebSite` was retired from 21 November 2024, and leftover markup "won't cause issues in Search" ([Google, Farewell sitelinks search box](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2024/10/sitelinks-search-box)).

Favicons follow the same root logic: a `<link rel="icon">` (or `shortcut icon`, `apple-touch-icon`, `apple-touch-icon-precomposed`) on the home page, one icon per hostname, a home page Googlebot can crawl, an icon file Googlebot-Image can crawl, and a stable URL that may live on a CDN ([Google, Favicon](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/favicon-in-search)). The icon must be square and at least 8×8 px, with "larger than 48x48px" recommended; the old "multiple of 48px" rule ended on 24 October 2024 ([Google, Favicon](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/favicon-in-search); [Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)). On **28 August 2026** Google began listing formats explicitly (BMP, GIF, ICO, PNG, JPEG, PPM and TIFF) and said the supported formats "haven't changed" ([Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)). **SVG is not on the list**, although the 2024 page allowed "SVG with a 1:1 (square) aspect ratio" ([Google favicon page, archived June 2024](http://web.archive.org/web/20240601170701/https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/favicon-in-search)); the two statements conflict, and the safe audit standard is at least one ICO or PNG icon declared on the root home page.

### Images need an img src, real alt text and a representative preferred image

**Google indexes images only from the `src` of `<img>`**, including inside `<picture>`, never from CSS backgrounds, in BMP, GIF, JPEG, PNG, WebP, SVG or AVIF ([Google, Image SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/google-images)). Alt text is "the most important attribute", filenames give "very light clues" and should be translated when images are localized, nearby text and captions inform the subject, and the same image should keep one URL across pages to spare crawl budget (added May 2025) ([Google, Image SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/google-images)). Lazy-loading that leaves `src` empty and parks the URL in `data-src` falls outside what Google says it reads. Since **2 March 2026** Google names three ways to nominate the thumbnail used in Search and Discover: schema.org `primaryImageOfPage`, an `image` attached to the main entity, or `og:image`, and it asks for a relevant, high-resolution image that is not a logo, not text-heavy and not extremely narrow or wide ([Google, Image SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/google-images)). For Discover, Google recommends images at least 1,200 px wide, enabled by `max-image-preview:large` ([Google, Discover](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/google-discover)). Licence metadata for the Licensable badge needs `contentUrl` plus one of `creator`, `creditText`, `copyrightNotice` or `license`, with `license` required for the badge itself; structured data wins over conflicting IPTC fields, and Google asks image pipelines to keep the IPTC creator, credit and copyright fields ([Google, Image metadata](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/image-license-metadata)).

### Core Web Vitals are three 75th-percentile thresholds and one signal among many

The Core Web Vitals are **LCP ≤ 2.5 s, INP ≤ 200 ms and CLS ≤ 0.1**, with "poor" above 4 s, 500 ms and 0.25, judged at the 75th percentile of page loads segmented by mobile and desktop, and a page passes only when all three pass ([web.dev, Defining the thresholds](https://web.dev/articles/defining-core-web-vitals-thresholds); [web.dev, Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)). INP replaced FID in March 2024, and all three metrics are "Stable", meaning they change at most once a year; none changed in 2025–2026 ([web.dev, Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)). Search Central words two boundaries as "less than 200 milliseconds" and "less than 0.1" ([Google, Core Web Vitals](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals)), but the web.dev table's "≤" is the formal definition. On ranking, Google says **"Core Web Vitals are used by our ranking systems"** yet "There is no single signal", other page-experience aspects "don't directly help your website rank higher", some assessments are site-wide, and relevant content still wins when experience is sub-par ([Google, Page experience](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/page-experience)).

### Open Graph needs four tags, and X falls back to them

The Open Graph protocol requires **`og:title`, `og:type`, `og:image` and `og:url`**, the last being "The canonical URL of your object that will be used as its permanent ID in the graph"; it lists `og:description`, `og:locale` (default `en_US`), `og:locale:alternate`, `og:site_name` and `og:determiner` as optional, says a page with `og:image` "should specify `og:image:alt`", gives the first of duplicate tags preference, and treats unmarked pages as type `website` ([Open Graph protocol](https://ogp.me/)). X's card markup reference, last captured in January 2026, requires `twitter:card` (`summary` or `summary_large_image` for web pages) and falls back to `og:title`, `og:description`, `og:image` and `og:image:alt`; images must be under 5 MB in JPG, PNG, WEBP or GIF and **never SVG**, 2:1 and at least 300×157 px for large cards or 1:1 and at least 144×144 px for summary cards, at most 4096×4096 px, with alt text up to 420 characters ([X, Cards markup, archived](http://web.archive.org/web/20260116124848/https://developer.x.com/en/docs/x-for-websites/cards/overview/markup); [X, Summary card with large image, archived](http://web.archive.org/web/20260112050218/https://developer.x.com/en/docs/x-for-websites/cards/overview/summary-card-with-large-image); [X, Summary card, archived](http://web.archive.org/web/20251216122910/https://developer.x.com/en/docs/x-for-websites/cards/overview/summary)). As of 12 November 2026 **those pages no longer exist on X's live developer site**, whose old URLs redirect to a general overview, so the archived text is the latest primary statement; the current Developer Policy still requires HTTPS for all card assets and bars misleading card content ([X Developer Policy](https://docs.x.com/developer-terms/policy)).

Google uses `og:title`, `og:site_name` and `og:image` as documented inputs, even though its list of supported meta tags omits Open Graph and says unsupported tags are ignored ([Google, Supported meta tags](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/special-tags)); that list is incomplete rather than authoritative on this point. Because the major link-preview scrapers sit on Next.js's HTML-limited list, they receive a blocking render with all metadata in the `<head>` ([Next.js html-bots.ts](https://github.com/vercel/next.js/blob/canary/packages/next/src/shared/lib/router/utils/html-bots.ts)).

## Structured data now buys rich results, not rankings or AI citations

### Eligibility rules demand visible, truthful, complete and crawlable markup

Google's structured-data rules are eligibility rules. Breaking them can draw a manual action that removes rich-result eligibility but **"doesn't affect how the page ranks in Google web search"**, after which Google ignores the page's markup ([Google, General structured data guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/sd-policies); [Google, Organization](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/organization)). JSON-LD is recommended, may sit in `<head>` or `<body>`, and can be injected by JavaScript, and Google calls its own documentation "definitive for Google Search behavior" over schema.org's ([Google, Intro to structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/intro-structured-data)). Injection carries documented costs: Google wants `Product` markup in the initial HTML because dynamic markup makes Shopping crawls "less frequent and less reliable" ([Google, Product snippet](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/product-snippet)), tag-manager duplication "increases the risk of having a mismatch" ([Google, Generate structured data with JavaScript](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/generate-structured-data-with-javascript)), non-200 and `noindex` pages may never be rendered, and markup past the 2 MB cut-off is never seen.

The content rules are the heart of it: "Don't mark up content that is not visible to readers of the page", and do not add structured data "about information that is not visible to the user, even if the information is accurate" ([Google, General guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/sd-policies); [Google, Intro to structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/intro-structured-data)). Markup must be "a true representation of the page content", include every required property, use the most specific type, repeat on duplicate pages, and include all reviews visible on the page; image URLs must be crawlable and indexable, and pages carrying markup must not be blocked ([Google, General guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/sd-policies)). The Manual Actions report names the violations an audit should test for, including "Structured data does not match content", "Structured data found on hidden content", list pages that aggregate "data from multiple items into one structured data element", "No mechanism for submitting a new review", reviews "written by the site or person providing the service", and events that are really promotions ([Google, Manual Actions report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044175)). Since **15 May 2026** the spam policies explicitly cover "attempting to manipulate generative AI responses in Google Search" ([Google, Spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies); [Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)).

### A marketplace may mark up provider reviews but never reviews of itself

The self-serving review rule is the one most specific to a marketplace. **When "the entity that's being reviewed controls the reviews about itself", its `LocalBusiness` or `Organization` pages are ineligible for stars**, whether the reviews sit in its own markup or arrive through an embedded widget, and ratings "must be sourced directly from users" rather than compiled by editors ([Google, Review snippet](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet)). Google's `LocalBusiness` table recommends `aggregateRating` and `review` "only ... for sites that capture reviews about other local businesses" ([Google, Local business](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business)), which describes a marketplace: customer reviews of independent providers, shown on provider pages, are the documented eligible use, while reviews of the marketplace itself on its own `Organization` are not. That reading is an inference, and it holds only while providers are genuinely separate businesses and the reviews come from their customers. The surrounding rules are strict: reviews must be visible and obvious, an `AggregateRating` must be visible, reviews must concern a specific item rather than a category, multiple reviews need an aggregate, ratings from other websites must not be aggregated, a review must identify its item in only one way (clarified 12 November 2025), Google recommends accepting ratings only with a comment and author name (15 January 2025), and since **24 July 2026** fake or undisclosed incentivized reviews are explicitly barred ([Google, Review snippet](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet); [Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)). A `Review` needs an `author` under 100 characters and a `reviewRating.ratingValue` using a dot for decimals; an `AggregateRating` needs `ratingValue` and either `ratingCount` or `reviewCount`, with `bestRating` defaulting to 5 ([Google, Review snippet](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet)). Google's 2019 FAQ told sites they did not need to remove self-serving reviews ([Google, Making review rich results more helpful](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2019/09/making-review-rich-results-more-helpful)), but the current documentation and the manual-action list are the controlling texts.

The table draws on Google's feature documentation ([Organization](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/organization); [Local business](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business); [Site names](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/site-names); [Breadcrumb](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/breadcrumb); [Review snippet](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet); [Carousel](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/carousel); [Structured data carousels (beta)](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/carousels-beta); [Event](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/event); [Profile page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/profile-page); [Article](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/article); [Product snippet](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/product-snippet); [Image metadata](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/image-license-metadata)), Google's posts on breadcrumbs and on simplifying results ([Google, January 2025](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/01/simplifying-breadcrumbs); [Google, August 2023](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/08/howto-faq-changes); [Google, June 2025](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/06/simplifying-search-results); [Google, November 2025](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2025/11/update-on-our-efforts)), the archived FAQ deprecation notice ([archived FAQPage documentation](http://web.archive.org/web/20260526013614/https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/faqpage)) and the changelog ([Google, Documentation updates](https://developers.google.com/search/updates)).

Two rows matter more for a Romanian marketplace than the table suggests. Romania is in the European Economic Area, so **the structured data carousels (beta), which accept `ItemList` markup of `LocalBusiness` items on a summary page, are open to its users**, and Google's EEA regional-differences page lists them among its aggregator features; on 18 September 2026 Google also extended its EEA aggregator and supplier units to local-business queries, the supplier unit being for direct providers such as "providers of services like plumbing" and needing no data "beyond what's accessible through web crawling" ([Google, Structured data carousels (beta)](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/carousels-beta); [Google, Regional differences](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/aggregator-features); [Google, Supplier unit](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/supplier-unit)). City or category pages that list at least three providers, each linking to its own detail page, fit the documented shape, though the interest forms mean eligibility is not automatic, and the manual-action rule against aggregating several items into one element bars a single combined rating on such pages. Provider pages, by contrast, fit `LocalBusiness` better than `ProfilePage`: Google built `ProfilePage` for people or organizations "affiliated with the overall website" and lists "An organization review site" as an invalid use ([Google, Profile page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/profile-page)). That conclusion is an inference, since Google never addresses marketplaces directly.

### schema.org defines the vocabulary while Google decides the feature

schema.org's current release is **V30.1 of 16 September 2026** ([schema.org, Releases](https://schema.org/docs/releases.html)). Its `addressCountry` recommends ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 codes and keeps alpha-3 codes or full country names "For backward compatibility" ([schema.org, addressCountry](https://schema.org/addressCountry)), while Google's Organization guidance asks for the two-letter code, so Romanian addresses should carry `RO` ([Google, Organization](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/organization)). `LocalBusiness` sits under both `Organization` and `Place`, with subtypes such as `HomeAndConstructionBusiness`, `HealthAndBeautyBusiness` and `ProfessionalService` ([schema.org, LocalBusiness](https://schema.org/LocalBusiness)), and `Organization` subtypes include `OnlineBusiness` ([schema.org, Organization](https://schema.org/Organization)), a candidate for the marketplace's own entity under Google's most-specific-subtype advice. `sameAs` takes a URL that "unambiguously indicates the item's identity" ([schema.org, sameAs](https://schema.org/sameAs)); `primaryImageOfPage` expects an `ImageObject`, although Google also accepts a plain URL ([schema.org, primaryImageOfPage](https://schema.org/primaryImageOfPage)); `BreadcrumbList` positions "should be integers, e.g. beginning with '1'" ([schema.org, BreadcrumbList](https://schema.org/BreadcrumbList)); and `CollectionPage` and `FAQPage` remain valid `WebPage` subtypes with no current Google feature ([schema.org, WebPage](https://schema.org/WebPage)). The working rule is that schema.org decides whether markup is valid vocabulary, and Google's pages decide whether it earns a feature.

### Structured data does not buy AI citations

Google says **"Structured data isn't required for generative AI search, and there's no special schema.org markup you need to add"**, while recommending it for rich results ([Google, AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)), and its snippet limits do not restrict structured data, which "remains usable for search results when declared within a `data-nosnippet` element" ([Google, Robots meta tag spec](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag)). Microsoft is warmer but hedged: structured data "may support clearer grounding but does not guarantee visibility or grounding traffic", and misleading markup "may be ignored" ([Bing guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a)); a 2025 conference remark that schema "helps Microsoft's LLMs understand your content" survives only as an attendee's quote ([Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/schema-llms-copilot-bing-microsoft-39093.html)). Apple documents the one AI-specific schema effect: pages marked `isAccessibleForFree: false` stay in search but are excluded from AI answer context ([Apple, About Applebot](https://support.apple.com/en-us/119829)). OpenAI, Anthropic and Perplexity publish nothing on structured data.

The measurements point the same way. In searchVIU's controlled test no assistant reported a price that existed only in JSON-LD, and conflicting values led to neither being reported ([searchVIU](https://www.searchviu.com/en/guides/schema-markup-ai/)). Ahrefs' matched study of 1,885 pages that added JSON-LD between August 2025 and March 2026 found **no meaningful citation uplift**: a small but statistically significant −4.6% in AI Overviews and statistically null changes of +2.4% in AI Mode and +2.2% in ChatGPT, even though cited pages were almost three times as likely to carry JSON-LD in the first place ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/schema-ai-citations/)). Every fact a marketplace wants quoted, from prices and opening hours to service areas and ratings, therefore has to exist as visible, server-rendered text; JSON-LD is an index-time supporting signal.

## AI engines split access into search, training and live-fetch switches

### Google's AI features ride on Search, with one new site-level opt-out

Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode retrieve from the Search index through "query fan-out" and retrieval-augmented generation, and a page qualifies as a supporting link when it is "indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet"; "There are no additional technical requirements" ([Google, AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features); [Google, AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). The July 2026 guide dismisses the AEO/GEO toolkit directly: llms.txt and Markdown copies are not used, "chunking" is unnecessary, special schema does not exist, and sites can "ignore tactics like 'chunking' content, creating unnecessary AI text files (like llms.txt), or pursuing inauthentic mentions" ([Google, AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)). What still matters is allowing crawling "in robots.txt, and by any CDN or hosting infrastructure", keeping important content in text, and matching structured data to visible text ([Google, AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). The same guide notes that browser agents read pages through "visual renderings (like screenshots)", the DOM and the accessibility tree ([Google, AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)).

Google now has four control layers, and only three of them touch Search. Googlebot's robots.txt rules govern all of Search, including AI features and Discover ([Google, Common crawlers](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-common-crawlers)), while `nosnippet`, `max-snippet` and `data-nosnippet` limit both snippets and "direct input" to AI Overviews and AI Mode, and `noindex` removes the page ([Google, Robots meta tag spec](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag)). The **Search generative AI control** in Search Console (Settings > Search generative AI), announced on 3 June 2026 and rolled out to all sites on **31 August 2026**, removes a property's links and content from AI Overviews, AI Mode and Discover's generative features within "a few days", without acting as a ranking signal elsewhere; inclusion is the default, properties inherit the setting from their closest changed parent, Google may still use the content to understand language, and the switch does not stop training ([Google, Search generative AI control](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16908024); [Google blog, New controls](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/new-controls-website-owners/)). **Google-Extended**, a robots.txt token with no crawler of its own, covers only Gemini Apps and Vertex AI training and grounding and "does not impact a site's inclusion in Google Search", so disallowing it leaves a site in AI Overviews; Google adds that it intends to honour the IETF AIPREF training signal (`user-agent: __usage_ai-training`) in early 2027 ([Google, Common crawlers](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-common-crawlers)). User-triggered fetchers such as Google-Agent and the Gemini Notebook fetcher "generally ignore robots.txt rules" ([Google, User-triggered fetchers](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawlers-fetchers/google-user-triggered-fetchers)). A generative AI performance report, also worldwide since 31 August 2026, counts how often a site's links were shown in AI Overviews and AI Mode ([Google, Generative AI performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/16984139)). Google's older "AI features and your website" page, last updated 10 December 2025, still calls Googlebot's robots.txt "the control"; the 2026 Help Center page and guide supersede it.

### Bing and Copilot share one index, and two tags Google dropped still bite there

Microsoft's rewritten Webmaster Guidelines cover "Bing search experiences, Copilot, and grounding API results" together and warn that ignoring them can mean "reduced eligibility for grounding experiences, or delisting from the Bing index" ([Bing guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a)). **`NOINDEX` removes a URL from all three surfaces; `NOARCHIVE` "prevents content from being used in Copilot responses and grounding results"; `NOCACHE` limits Copilot to URL, title and snippet; `NOSNIPPET` and `data-nosnippet` stop captions and "may limit Copilot citation quality"**; and "robots.txt controls crawl access, not indexing" ([Bing guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a)). Those meanings date from Bing's 2023 chat controls, which also tied both tags to training use ([Bing, 2023 announcement](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2023/9/Announcing-new-options-for-webmasters-to-control-usage-of-their-content-in-Bing-Chat/)), so a `noarchive` left in a template, which Google has ignored since 2024, quietly removes a site from Copilot answers. The guidelines also mention a "data-snippet attribute" that no other Bing page documents; it reads like a typo for `data-nosnippet`, and the page exposes no date, so the 2026 rewrite cannot be dated precisely.

On content, Microsoft asks for explicit facts, essential information "near the top of the URL" and no long introductions, lists cloaking and "Prompt Injection and AI Manipulation" as abuses, and states that "GEO does not guarantee grounding or citations in AI experiences"; for freshness it wants accurate `lastmod`, ETags and streaming rather than batch IndexNow submissions ([Bing guidelines](https://www.bing.com/webmasters/help/webmaster-guidelines-30fba23a)). Its December 2025 note that LLMs "group near-duplicate URLs into a single cluster" and may select "a version that is outdated or not the one you intended to highlight" bears directly on template-translated English and Romanian pages ([Bing, Duplicate content and AI search](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2025/12/Does-Duplicate-Content-Hurt-SEO-and-AI-Search-Visibility/)). Bing Webmaster Tools has reported citations, cited pages and "grounding queries" since its February 2026 AI Performance preview, adding intents, citation share and topics in June and October 2026, and the launch post says "Clear headings, tables, and FAQ sections help surface key information" ([Bing, AI Performance](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2026/2/Introducing-AI-Performance-in-Bing-Webmaster-Tools-Public-Preview/); [Bing, June 2026](https://blogs.bing.com/search/2026/6/New-AI-Visibility-Insights-in-Bing-Webmaster-Tools-Intents-Topics-Citation-Share-Compare/); [Bing, October 2026](https://blogs.bing.com/webmaster/2026/10/introducing-topics-portfolio-beta-in-ai-performance/)), a reminder that visible FAQ content still has a job after Google's FAQ rich result disappeared. Copilot's live fetches carried no unique user agent in third-party tests, so robots.txt cannot single them out ([Vercel/MERJ](https://vercel.com/blog/the-rise-of-the-ai-crawler); [Writesonic](https://writesonic.com/blog/ai-crawler-study-what-llms-see-on-your-website)), and Microsoft has published nothing on llms.txt.

The rows come from each operator's crawler documentation ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots); [Anthropic](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8896518-does-anthropic-crawl-data-from-the-web-and-how-can-site-owners-block-the-crawler); [Perplexity](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/resources/perplexity-crawlers); [Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/119829); [Common Crawl](https://commoncrawl.org/ccbot)).

The details change the audit. OpenAI asks sites to allow `OAI-SearchBot` in robots.txt and its **published IP ranges** at the firewall, says one crawl may serve both search and training when both bots are allowed, and lists a fourth agent, `OAI-AdsBot`, that visits only pages submitted as ChatGPT ads ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/bots)); its publisher FAQ blocked automated retrieval, so claims attributed to it in secondary summaries, such as `noindex` keeping pages out of ChatGPT search and `utm_source=chatgpt.com` on referral links, remain unverified. **Anthropic is the only operator that says all its agents, including the user-triggered one, honour robots.txt**; it also respects CAPTCHAs, warns that IP blocking can stop it reading robots.txt and so fail as an opt-out, asks for rules on every subdomain, and since **30 October 2026** recognizes an AIPREF-based "no AI training" signal, with any robots.txt rule naming `ClaudeBot` taking precedence ([Anthropic](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/8896518-does-anthropic-crawl-data-from-the-web-and-how-can-site-owners-block-the-crawler)). Perplexity contradicts itself, saying both that `Perplexity-User` "controls which sites these user requests can access" and that it "generally ignores robots.txt rules", and it warns that Cloudflare's Bot Fight Mode can challenge its bots even when a WAF rule allows them ([Perplexity](https://docs.perplexity.ai/docs/resources/perplexity-crawlers)); Cloudflare accused Perplexity in August 2025 of using "stealth, undeclared crawlers" after network blocks, an accusation Perplexity disputes ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/perplexity-is-using-stealth-undeclared-crawlers-to-evade-website-no-crawl-directives/)).

Apple lets `nosnippet` keep content out of Siri and Search "broad world knowledge answers" and `isAccessibleForFree: false` keep a page out of AI answer context, and it warns that blocking scripts or CSS through robots.txt can stop Applebot rendering ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-us/119829)); because Applebot follows `Googlebot` rules whenever robots.txt does not name it, every Googlebot-specific rule is also an Apple rule. Common Crawl warns of crawlers "falsely identifying themselves as CCBot" and publishes IP ranges for verification ([Common Crawl](https://commoncrawl.org/ccbot)). The pattern for a site that wants citations is to allow each engine's search and user-fetch agents at both robots.txt and the edge, and to treat training tokens (`GPTBot`, `ClaudeBot`, `Applebot-Extended`, `Google-Extended`, `CCBot`) as a separate business decision with no documented effect on citation. One second-hand finding suggests Bing indexation also matters for ChatGPT, with 87% of sampled ChatGPT search citations matching Bing's top organic results ([Advanced Web Ranking](https://www.advancedwebranking.com/blog/what-ai-search-crawlers-read)), but OpenAI documents no such dependency.

### Cloudflare can block Googlebot while robots.txt says allow

Cloudflare's AI controls moved through five stages. September 2024 brought a one-click AI-bot block; **1 July 2025** brought a sign-up prompt asking new domains whether to allow AI crawlers, managed robots.txt rules prepended to the site's own file, and pay-per-crawl, which answers unpaid AI requests with `402 Payment Required` ([Cloudflare press release](https://www.cloudflare.com/press/press-releases/2025/cloudflare-just-changed-how-ai-crawlers-scrape-the-internet-at-large/); [Cloudflare, Pay per crawl](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-crawl-control/features/pay-per-crawl/what-is-pay-per-crawl/)); 24 September 2025 brought a Content Signals Policy with `search`, `ai-input` and `ai-train` values, served by default as the robots.txt of Free-plan zones that have none ([Cloudflare, Content Signals Policy](https://blog.cloudflare.com/content-signals-policy/); [Cloudflare, robots.txt setting](https://developers.cloudflare.com/bots/additional-configurations/managed-robots-txt/)); 1 July 2026 brought separate Search, Agent and Training policies, with "Verified" bot status no longer meaning allowed by default ([Cloudflare, Your site, your rules](https://blog.cloudflare.com/content-independence-day-ai-options/)). Then on **15 September 2026** came the change that matters most: **"Block" and "Block on pages with ads" for Training now apply to mixed-use crawlers, explicitly Googlebot, Bingbot and Applebot**, so that setting "will stop Applebot, Bingbot, and Googlebot from reaching your site — search included" ([Cloudflare, Have it both ways](https://blog.cloudflare.com/accountable-mixed-use-ai-crawlers/)).

The setting that stops training while keeping search is **"Disallow AI Training"**, which publishes a no-training preference through Bot Preference Sync (the successor to managed robots.txt) and blocks training-only crawlers at the edge; it does not yet convey anything to Bing, whose robots.txt no-training support Cloudflare expects in early 2027, a date Microsoft has not confirmed ([Cloudflare, Have it both ways](https://blog.cloudflare.com/accountable-mixed-use-ai-crawlers/); [Cloudflare, AI Crawler and Agent access policies](https://developers.cloudflare.com/bots/additional-configurations/block-ai-bots/)). The policies sit under Security > Settings, filtered by Bot traffic ([Cloudflare, AI Crawler and Agent access policies](https://developers.cloudflare.com/bots/additional-configurations/block-ai-bots/)). Cloudflare's own sources disagree on new-domain defaults (the current docs say Allow for all three behaviours, the July 2026 post predicted ad-page blocks, and the September post describes an ads-based preset), so only a zone's live settings settle the question. The managed file adds `Content-signal` lines that Search Console "may occasionally report" as "Syntax not understood", which Cloudflare says has had no observed effect on crawling ([Cloudflare, robots.txt setting](https://developers.cloudflare.com/bots/additional-configurations/managed-robots-txt/)), and AI Crawl Control can now 301-redirect AI training crawlers that request a non-canonical URL to its canonical while humans and search crawlers see the page unchanged ([Cloudflare, AI Crawl Control changelog](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-crawl-control/changelog/)). For an audit, **a Training policy of "Block" on a Cloudflare zone is an indexing emergency hidden behind a clean robots.txt**: it removes the site from Google Search, AI Overviews, AI Mode, Bing, Copilot and Apple search at once.

### llms.txt is optional, ignored by Google and rarely fetched

The llms.txt proposal, first published in September 2024 and revised as "v2" in August 2026, suggests a Markdown file at `/llms.txt` or any subpath, Markdown mirrors of pages, and `rel="alternate" type="text/markdown"` and `rel="describedby"` links, for use "on demand" by agents rather than for training ([llmstxt.org](https://llmstxt.org/)). **Google Search ignores it**, stating that creating one "will neither harm nor help your site's visibility or rankings" ([Google, AI optimization guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide)), while Chrome's Lighthouse checks for one in its experimental agentic-browsing audits and marks a 404 "Not Applicable" because the file is "optional at the moment" ([Lighthouse, llms.txt audit](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/lighthouse/agentic-browsing/llms-txt)). None of OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity, Microsoft or Apple says its crawler or answer engine reads the file. Ahrefs' log study of 137,210 domains found that 97% of published llms.txt files received no requests at all in May 2026, that GPTBot and Claude Code led the few fetches, and that the search crawlers of OpenAI, Perplexity and Anthropic made "only a couple of hundred fetches across thousands of sites" ([Ahrefs, llms.txt study](https://ahrefs.com/blog/llmstxt-study/)). For a consumer marketplace the file is optional, and any Markdown mirror that is published must match the HTML, or it starts to resemble the cloaking Bing penalizes.

## Conclusion

The unit of truth for this audit has moved from what a browser shows to what each bot receives through the production edge. Three of the period's sharpest failure modes are invisible in a rendered DOM: truncation at 2 MB of uncompressed HTML, canonicals, `noindex` tags and not-found states that depend on JavaScript a crawler may never run, and edge policies that block Googlebot while robots.txt says allow. A fourth, the out-of-order `hidden` segments that React streaming sends to every AI fetcher the framework does not recognize, is invisible even in DevTools, because the browser reassembles the page. The evidence an audit needs is therefore raw responses fetched with real user agents (Googlebot Smartphone, Bingbot, Applebot, OAI-SearchBot, ChatGPT-User, Claude-SearchBot, Claude-User, PerplexityBot) through the live CDN, checked for status, `<head>` order, byte position of the main text and whether that text sits inside a `hidden` element. The costliest errors in the AI layer are category errors rather than syntax errors: disallowing Google-Extended in the belief that it removes AI Overviews, choosing Cloudflare's Training "Block" and losing Google Search, leaving a Google-dead `noarchive` that deletes the site from Copilot, or expecting robots.txt to stop the user-triggered fetchers that OpenAI, Perplexity and Google say may ignore it.

For a bilingual marketplace, the decisive levers are content rather than tags. Hreflang has become forgiving (case-insensitive, with broken pairs dropped one at a time), but whether the English pages exist in Google as documents of their own depends on whether provider content is actually translated, and whether any engine quotes a price, an opening hour or a rating depends on that fact being visible server-rendered text rather than JSON-LD. Google's structured-data catalogue keeps shrinking toward a core of Organization, LocalBusiness, reviews of other businesses, desktop breadcrumbs, physical events and profiles, which pushes markup effort onto provider pages and listing pages; for a Romanian site, the EEA-only carousels and the September 2026 extension of aggregator units to local businesses are the one place where `ItemList` markup of providers has a payoff a non-European marketplace would not get. Training controls, meanwhile, are converging on the IETF AIPREF signal, which Anthropic honours now and Google plans for early 2027, with Cloudflare expecting Microsoft to follow, so the durable posture is to allow search and live-fetch agents everywhere, decide training separately, and re-verify the edge after every vendor change.
